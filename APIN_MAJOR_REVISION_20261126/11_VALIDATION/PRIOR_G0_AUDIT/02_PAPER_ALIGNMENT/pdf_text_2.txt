Abstract
Evaluating highway speed forecasts is challenging where fixed roadside sens-
ing is sparse and commercial floating car data (FCD) are incomplete. When
FCD are obtained through an application programming interface (API), planned
observations may be omitted rather than returned as explicit missing val-
ues, while model comparisons can be biased by unequal evaluation samples
or temporally non-causal preprocessing. We address these issues with the
Grid-Reconstructed, Auditable, Temporally Causal, and Eligibility-Gated Multi-
Horizon FCD Forecasting (GATE-FCD) framework. GATE-FCD reconstructs
the planned observation grid, applies explicit eligibility rules, freezes common
sample manifests, and restricts preprocessing to information available before each
forecast origin. Historical Average, Seasonal Historical Average (SeasonalHA),
Persistence, Ridge, XGBoost, and a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) are evaluated
at 51 virtual nodes using 24-h histories, six retrospective rolling-origin folds,
and 1-, 3-, and 6-h future-window targets (H1, H3, and H6). XGBoost achieved
the lowest descriptive mean absolute error (MAE) at H1, H3, and H6 (0.949,

0.829, and 0.752 km h −1, respectively), although SeasonalHA remained compet-
itive and fold-level inference did not establish a stable advantage. These findings
highlight the importance of auditable sample construction and temporally causal
evaluation when benchmarking forecasting models with incomplete commercial
FCD.

1 Introduction
Reliable speed forecasts support traffic management, route guidance, and other intel-
ligent transportation system functions. Long-distance highways present a particular
sensing problem because continuous roadside detection is expensive to deploy and
maintain over large spatial extents. Floating car data (FCD) offer a practical alter-
native by providing speed and travel-time information without a dense network of
fixed detectors. Recent work has used FCD for speed estimation, congestion analysis,
traffic-state monitoring, and forecasting [20]. Commercial FCD have also been com-
bined with lower-cost sensing sources where conventional monitoring is limited [11].
The remaining question for forecasting is not simply whether FCD are available, but
whether the observations returned by a commercial service can be converted into a
consistent and auditable time series.
Commercial FCD differ from conventional detector data in several ways that mat-
ter for evaluation. Services such as TomTom provide provider-processed speed and
travel-time estimates through an application programming interface (API) rather than
raw vehicle trajectories, leaving upstream sampling, map matching, aggregation, and
filtering outside the user’s control. More importantly, a planned node–time observation
may be absent from the API response altogether rather than appearing as an explicit
null. The Pan Borneo Highway dataset used here illustrates this problem: 51 fixed
virtual nodes cover an approximately 130-km corridor, but observations are spatially
discrete and their temporal coverage, confidence, and gap lengths vary by node and
time. If returned rows are treated as a complete series, unreturned observations disap-
pear from the denominator and the distinction among missing, quality-ineligible, and
imputed values becomes unclear. That ambiguity can change which forecast origins
are available to each model.
Prior research addresses the main components of this problem, but usually in
separate workflows. Commercial and vehicle-level FCD have been used for traffic-
state estimation and prediction [11, 18]. Multi-horizon studies have compared naive,
statistical, machine-learning, and deep-learning models and have shown that relative
performance can change with forecast horizon [8, 19]. A separate body of work treats
incomplete traffic observations through completion, imputation, or joint prediction–
imputation methods [1, 3, 15, 21]. These strands provide the necessary methods, but
they do not by themselves ensure that sparse API-derived FCD are evaluated on a fixed

observation space, with the same forecast cases and the same temporal information
boundaries across models.
Applying these methods to sparse commercial FCD exposes three evaluation
problems. Planned node–time combinations must first be reconstructed before com-
pleteness and eligibility can be measured. Model comparisons can otherwise be
confounded if different predictors retain different origins or use different missing-value
treatments. Temporal order also has to be enforced inside preprocessing: a chronolog-
ical train–test split does not prevent leakage if normalization, imputation, or feature
statistics use information unavailable at the forecast origin [9]. Finally, one test inter-
val and one mean error cannot show whether a small model advantage persists across
time, locations, forecast horizons, or history-reliability conditions. When differences
between simple and complex models are small, stability under common causal samples
is more informative than the mean ranking alone.
The analysis addresses three questions: (i) can sparse API-derived FCD support 1-,
3-, and 6-h future-window mean-speed forecasting after explicit observation and tar-
get eligibility checks; (ii) how do Historical Average (HA), Seasonal Historical Average
(SeasonalHA), Persistence, Ridge, XGBoost, and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) com-
pare when they use identical forecast origins and target definitions; and (iii) how stable
are those comparisons across rolling-origin folds, random seeds, and history-reliability
conditions? We address these questions with the Grid-Reconstructed, Auditable,
Temporally Causal, and Eligibility-Gated Multi-Horizon FCD Forecasting framework
(GATE-FCD). GATE-FCD reconstructs the planned node–time grid, distinguishes
missing and quality-ineligible observations, creates a causal 24-h history, and freezes
a common sample manifest before model fitting. Six models are then evaluated across
six retrospective rolling-origin folds and three horizons. This design isolates differences
in forecasting behavior from differences in sample composition and makes it possible
to examine where a small accuracy gain is stable and where it reverses.
Section 2 reviews FCD forecasting, model complexity, and incomplete-data evalu-
ation. Section 3 defines the corridor, reconstructed observation grid, eligibility rules,
and causal features; Section 4 specifies the three targets and six models; and Section 5
reports the rolling-origin, statistical, stability, and reliability analyses. Section 6 sum-
marizes the findings and their evidence limits. Detailed audit rules, model settings,
and sensitivity results are provided in the appendices.
2 Related work
2.1 FCD-based traffic forecasting
FCD are now used for traffic-state estimation as well as for travel-time and speed
forecasting. Their main practical advantage is spatial coverage without the installation
of a dense fixed-sensor network. Zhang et al. [20] reviewed applications spanning speed
estimation, travel-time analysis, congestion identification, and traffic-state monitoring.
Ipekyuz et al. [11] combined commercial FCD with Bluetooth observations on urban
arterial links, showing how provider data can complement limited roadside sensing.
For forecasting, however, usefulness depends on the observation structure supplied to
the analyst, not only on nominal data availability.
3
PAGE 4
That structure differs substantially across FCD sources. Taxi or fleet global posi-
tioning system (GPS) data retain vehicle-level trajectories and can describe individual
movements in space and time. Commercial services such as HERE and TomTom
instead expose processed traffic states for road segments or predefined locations, typ-
ically as aggregated speed, travel time, and provider-specific quality fields. Users of
these services do not directly control the upstream sampling or map-matching process.
Yu et al. [18] used taxi trajectories with an Autoformer-based model, whereas Hadry
et al. [8] evaluated multiple forecasting methods using HERE traffic information. The
difference is consequential: trajectory-level datasets preserve information that is not
available when the input consists of provider-aggregated states.
Most published FCD forecasting experiments also use urban networks, dense obser-
vation structures, or regular time series. A long regional highway with fixed virtual
nodes has a different sampling geometry. In the present corridor, observations are
discrete and an API call may return no record for a planned node–time slot. Before
forecasting models can be compared, the analysis must first establish which observa-
tions should have existed, which were actually returned, and which satisfy the quality
rules used to construct an hourly series.
2.2 Multi-horizon traffic forecasting and model complexity
Forecast horizon changes the information available to a predictor. Persistence can
be strong over short intervals because the current state remains informative, while
longer horizons place greater weight on recurring temporal patterns and relationships
learned from earlier observations. Hadry et al. [8] reported horizon-dependent dif-
ferences among traffic and context-based predictors, and Zeb et al. [19] developed
horizon-specific feature representations for multi-step forecasting. These findings make
H1, H3, and H6 distinct evaluation tasks even when they are generated from the same
underlying traffic series.
Recent models use increasingly flexible architectures to capture nonlinear and
spatiotemporal dependence. Cini and Aydin [6] combined convolutional neural net-
work (CNN), long short-term memory (LSTM), and GRU components for long-term
traffic-flow prediction. Liu and Zhang [13] used a spatial–temporal Transformer with
temporal aggregation and spatial memory, while Zeb et al. [19] adapted feature rep-
resentations to different forecast steps. Such models can represent relationships that
a simple baseline cannot, but they also introduce more fitted parameters, training
choices, and sensitivity to data scale. Their practical value is clearer when they are
tested against strong low-complexity references on exactly the same forecast cases.
The six models used here span different assumptions rather than a progression
toward a presumed best architecture. HA represents the overall training-period level,
SeasonalHA recurring calendar structure, and Persistence the continuation of the lat-
est model-ready speed. Ridge asks whether the common history, reliability, calendar,
and node features are informative through an approximately linear relation. XGBoost
allows nonlinear interactions among the same tabular features, and GRU tests whether
a recurrent representation of the 24-h sequence adds useful predictive information.
Because the corridor contains 51 discrete virtual nodes rather than a dense sensor

graph, the experiment does not assume that a more elaborate spatial–temporal archi-
tecture is inherently favored. The relevant test is whether added flexibility produces
a repeatable gain after sample composition and target definition have been fixed.
2.3 Forecasting with incomplete and reliability-varying traffic
data
Incomplete traffic observations are often handled by reconstructing or imputing val-
ues before forecasting. Chen et al. [3] used low-rank tensor completion for incomplete
spatiotemporal traffic data, Wang et al. [16] reformulated traffic imputation through
an image-restoration representation, and Bikram et al. [1] linked dynamic spatiotem-
poral imputation with speed prediction. Qu et al. [15] jointly modeled prediction and
imputation, while Xu et al. [17] considered probabilistic network-wide prediction and
imputation. Reviews by Zhang et al. [21] and Chan et al. [2] show that the effects of
missing traffic data depend on the missingness pattern and on the spatial and temporal
information available for recovery.
Commercial API-derived FCD introduce a step that precedes numerical imputa-
tion. Many missing-data methods start with an established sensor–time matrix in
which an empty cell is already visible. An API may simply omit an expected node–time
record. If completeness is calculated only from returned rows, both the denominator
and the admissible forecast origins can change with the response itself. The intended
node–time grid must therefore be reconstructed before an unreturned slot can be
distinguished from a returned record that fails a quality condition.
Temporal causality creates a second constraint. Hewamalage et al. [9] describe how
time-series evaluation can remain biased even with chronological train and test periods
when preprocessing uses later information. In the present setting, scalers, fallback
statistics, rolling features, and target construction all have to respect the forecast
origin. Model comparability adds another constraint: if a GRU, a tree model, and
a baseline retain different origins because of their input requirements, differences in
reported error no longer reflect model behavior alone. A common forecast manifest is
therefore needed to hold sample IDs and target definitions fixed across predictors.
Reliability also varies from one forecast origin to another. Two 24-h histories can
contain the same number of rows but differ in observed coverage, the length of a con-
secutive gap, the age of the latest genuine observation, or the amount of causal filling.
Work on probabilistic prediction and missing-data evaluation shows why incomplete
histories should not be summarized only by a global missing rate [2, 17, 21]. For
sparse commercial FCD, model ranking is most interpretable when four conditions are
controlled together: omitted API slots are restored to a fixed observation space, pre-
processing remains causal, all models share the same eligible forecast cases, and errors
are examined against the reliability of the pre-origin history. GATE-FCD combines
these controls in one evaluation protocol; it does not assume that model complexity
itself resolves deficiencies in the observation process.

3 Dataset and preprocessing
3.1 Study corridor and FCD collection
The study focuses on an approximately 130-km corridor of the Pan Borneo Highway
in Sarawak, Malaysia. The Pan Borneo Highway is a major interregional road sys-
tem that supports connectivity and economic activities across East Malaysia [12, 14].
This corridor was selected to examine traffic forecasting in a regional-highway envi-
ronment where dense fixed roadside sensing is limited. Unlike a dense urban sensor
network, the study corridor is represented by spatially discrete FCD observation loca-
tions distributed along the highway mainline. This setting provides a practical case
for evaluating whether commercial FCD can support multi-horizon speed forecasting
when continuous roadside measurements are not available.
The corridor contains 51 fixed virtual observation nodes, denoted N01–N51 and
numbered sequentially along the mainline. Where practicable, the nodes were posi-
tioned away from side roads and complex intersections to reduce local disturbances
that are not representative of the mainline traffic state. Figure 1 shows the spa-
tial distribution of the nodes and the spacing between adjacent observations. The 51
nodes define 50 adjacent-node intervals and correspond to an average spatial density
of approximately 0.39 nodes km −1. The mean adjacent-node spacing is 2.60 km, the
median is 2.81 km, the interquartile range is 2.34 km, and the maximum spacing is
4.35 km. Node spacing is non-uniform along the corridor. These nodes should be inter-
preted as discrete observation locations rather than continuous sensing of the road
between adjacent nodes.
Traffic observations were obtained from the TomTom Traffic API. The service pro-
vides provider-processed traffic information at predefined virtual locations rather than
raw trajectories of individual vehicles. The available records include current speed,
free-flow speed, current and free-flow travel time, confidence information, and road-
closure status. The forecasting target isFCD-reported speed, which represents the
speed estimated by the commercial provider from its upstream data and processing
pipeline. It should not be interpreted as an independently measured ground-truth
speed from a fixed detector. This distinction is retained throughout the study because
the forecasting experiment evaluates the temporal predictability of the provider-
reported traffic state rather than the measurement accuracy of the FCD service
itself.
The raw FCD records span from 8 December 2025 at 13:15 to 28 February 2026 at
23:45 in the Asia/Kuala Lumpur time zone (UTC+08:00), with a planned temporal
resolution of 15 min. The first raw records occur within the incomplete 13:00–14:00
boundary hour on 8 December 2025. This partial starting hour is retained in the raw-
data provenance but excluded from the formal complete-bin panel used for hourly
forecasting. Accordingly, the complete analysis grid begins at 14:00 on 8 December
2025 and extends through the final complete hour beginning at 23:00 on 28 February
2026. The construction of this complete temporal grid, its theoretical denominator,
and the eligibility rules applied before hourly aggregation are defined in Section 3.2.

3.2 Complete-grid reconstruction and data eligibility
Commercial FCD APIs return observed records rather than an explicit row for every
planned node–time combination. Returned rows alone cannot be used as the denomi-
nator for completeness. Raw timestamps were expressed in Asia/Kuala Lumpur time
(UTC+08:00) and floored to 15-min intervals. The raw series starts at 13:15 on 8
December 2025, but the 13:00–14:00 boundary hour is incomplete. The formal com-
plete grid starts at 14:00 on 8 December 2025 and ends at 23:45 on 28 February
2026.
The complete period contains 7,912 planned 15-min intervals and 1,978 complete
hourly intervals per node. With 51 fixed nodes, the two theoretical denominators are
N15 = 51×7,912 = 403,512, N hour = 51×1,978 = 100,878.(1)
Every planned node–quarter-hour combination remains in the reconstructed grid
even when no usable API record was returned. An unreturned observation is
represented explicitly as missing rather than disappearing from the denominator.
Returned records were screened before aggregation. A record was retained only
when the frozen timestamp, coordinate, speed, travel-time, confidence, and general-
validity checks passed; road closure was false; current travel time was finite and
positive; speed satisfied 0< v≤130 km h −1; and provider confidence satisfied
0.80≤c≤1.00. The raw input contained 244,477 records. Of these, 1,170 failed the
confidence threshold, while none failed the positive-speed rule or exceeded 130 km h−1.
The complete frozen filter retained 243,307 records. No separate provider-status

The six rolling-origin folds are the primary paired inferential units; node–time rows
within a test period are not treated as independent replications. Dependence-aware
forecast comparison should use paired evaluation units rather than treating correlated
forecast errors as independent replications [9]. For modelsAandB, the study-specific
paired fold-level effect is defined as
∆(f,H)A,B = MAEA,f,H −MAE B,f,H ,(14)
so negative values favor modelA. Five comparisons were prespecified within
each horizon: XGBoost–GRU, XGBoost–Ridge, XGBoost–SeasonalHA, Persistence–
HA, and GRU–Ridge. For each comparison, the frozen pipeline applies a two-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank test to the six paired fold-level MAE differences and uses 10,000
paired-bootstrap resamples to form an unadjusted 95% confidence interval. The five
p-values are then adjusted separately within each horizon using the Holm procedure
[7], yielding 15 prespecified comparisons in total. With only six paired folds per hori-
zon, non-significance is interpreted as insufficient evidence of a stable difference rather
than proof of equivalence.
A supplementary dependence-aware analysis uses 10,000 day×spatial-block boot-
strap replicates. Forty-one test days are crossed with five spatial blocks, producing
205 day–block clusters. Because this estimator changes the weighting and dependence
structure relative to the fold-macro analysis, it is treated as supplementary uncertainty
evidence rather than a second winner-selection procedure [9].

5.6 Reliability-conditioned forecasting performance
The overall ranking changes when forecasts are separated by the reliability of their
input history. Figure 5(c) forms horizon-specific, error-blind quartiles from the pre-
origin history-coverage ratio. At H1, the lowest-coverage quartile Q1 gives a fold-macro

available before prediction.
H3 and H6 do not follow a simple monotonic pattern. At H3, XGBoost/SeasonalHA

switching threshold. This non-monotonicity also shows why one global model ranking
is insufficient for reliability-varying histories.

Model choice in this experiment depends on both horizon and history reliability.
Persistence remains a useful H1 reference but loses value at H3 and H6. SeasonalHA
stays competitive across horizons and is particularly strong in some lower-coverage
conditions. XGBoost has the lowest descriptive mean error and its clearest H1 advan-
tage occurs in the high-coverage group, yet the average gap from SeasonalHA is small
compared with the variation across folds. In an operational pipeline, data eligibility
should be checked before model selection; additional model complexity should not be
used to compensate for an unreliable history. Cases that fail the history or target
requirements are better flagged, assigned lower confidence, or rejected.
5.7 Threats to validity
The findings are specific to the spatial and temporal scope of the experiment. The
study covers one approximately 130-km Pan Borneo Highway corridor, 51 virtual
nodes, and roughly three months of observations. Six rolling-origin test periods provide
repeated out-of-time evidence, but they do not represent year-round seasonality, rare
disruptions, or the full range of corridor traffic regimes. Relative model rankings and
reliability patterns require confirmation on longer records and additional corridors.

of pseudoreplication, the 92,147 test rows are not treated as independent inferential

6 Conclusion
GATE-FCD was developed to make multi-horizon forecasting from sparse commercial
FCD auditable before model rankings are interpreted. The framework reconstructs
planned node–time combinations, separates missing from quality-ineligible records,
builds causal 24-h histories, freezes common forecast samples, and checks the resulting
predictions against leakage and consistency rules. The formal experiment produced
18 fold–horizon manifests and 180 completed model runs. All 20 full-sample audit
assertions passed, with no detected future-information violation, target-recomputation
mismatch, or cross-split target-window violation.
On these common samples, XGBoost had the lowest descriptive fold-macro MAE
at H1, H3, and H6: 0.949, 0.829, and 0.752 km h −1. SeasonalHA was close at 0.961,
0.853, and 0.774 km h −1. Persistence was competitive at H1 but deteriorated at H3
and H6, while GRU and Ridge did not provide a stable gain that matched their
additional modeling complexity under the present data scale and protocol. Because
each horizon is a different future-window mean, these error magnitudes are interpreted
within horizon rather than as evidence that the longer task is easier.
The descriptive XGBoost ranking was not statistically stable. Its mean MAE dif-
ferences from SeasonalHA were only−0.0117,−0.0235, and−0.0222 km h −1; all
three fold-level intervals crossed zero, and none of the 15 prespecified comparisons
was significant after Holm correction. Rankings also reversed across test folds and
history-coverage strata. At H1, SeasonalHA was better in the lowest-coverage quar-
tile, whereas XGBoost was better in the highest-coverage quartile. No single model
can be treated as the preferred choice across all horizons and reliability states.
These conclusions remain bounded by one corridor, one commercial FCD source,
an approximately three-month record, provider-reported speed targets, and retrospec-
tive rolling-origin evaluation. Prospective testing, independent reference speeds, longer
and cross-corridor datasets, additional providers and exogenous variables, and mea-
sured deployment costs are needed before broader generalization. GATE-FCD places
data eligibility before model choice: a forecast case should first satisfy the observa-
tion, history, target, and temporal-causality requirements; only then should additional
model complexity be judged against the required horizon and reliability condition.

aggregated to hourly speed under the 2-of-4 rule. A 24-row history{t 0 −23, . . . , t0}
is then constructed for each candidate origin using causal forward fill followed, when
necessary, by fold-training node-median and corridor-median fallback. Targets begin
att 0 + 1 and use no imputation. H1 requires 1/1 observed future hour, H3 requires
3/3, and the primary H6 definition requires at least 5/6.
The final manifest audit applied the following 20 assertions to 1,053,618 manifest
records; every assertion passed with zero violations:
1. sample identifiers are unique within the audited manifest structure;
2. each model history contains exactly 24 hourly rows;
3. history timestamps are strictly increasing;
4. every history timestamp is at or before its forecast origin;
5. every target timestamp is strictly after its forecast origin;
6. the complete target window remains inside the assigned split;
7. target values are never imputed;
8. input history and target windows do not overlap;
9. node identity is consistent across history, origin, and target;
10. targets can be recomputed from the recorded source row identifiers;
11. H1 requires 1 of 1 observed future hour;
12. H3 requires 3 of 3 observed future hours;
13. H6 requires at least 5 of 6 observed future hours;
14. scaler parameters are fitted from fold-training data only;
15. node and corridor fallback medians are fitted from fold-training data only;
16. any regime threshold used by the pipeline is training-only or not used in the formal
benchmark;
17. backward filling is absent;
18. centered rolling statistics are absent;
19. future-data merges are absent; and
20. all compared models use the same common sample manifest within each fold and
horizon.
The audit also verifies one-to-one matching between predictions and manifest rows,
prediction-file and manifest hashes, the 180-run registry, and the frozen configura-
tion lineage. These checks support internal reproducibility but do not convert the
retrospective design into an independent blind test.

C Supplementary metrics and sensitivity analyses
Table 9 reports the six-fold means of RMSE, sMAPE, and Bias. These metrics are sec-
ondary to MAE but provide checks on large errors, relative error scale, and systematic
signed error. The broad ordering is consistent with the primary analysis: XGBoost
and SeasonalHA remain the leading pair at H3 and H6, while Persistence deteriorates
substantially with horizon.
The supplementary day×spatial-block bootstrap used 10,000 replicates, 41 test
days, and five spatial blocks. For XGBoost minus SeasonalHA, the estimates were
+0.068, +0.025, and +0.023 km h −1 at H1, H3, and H6, with 95% intervals
[−0.151,0.376], [−0.112,0.189], and [−0.094,0.211]. All intervals include zero. Their
signs differ from the fold-macro means because the cluster estimator applies a different
weighting and dependence structure.
